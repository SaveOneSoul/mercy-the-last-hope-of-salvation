Brain, habits, consciousness & moral agency

This visual study map connects major brain systems with pleasure, pain, habits and awareness while keeping a crucial boundary: neuroscience can describe mechanisms that influence behaviour, but it cannot identify a “sin centre,” measure guilt, or replace moral and spiritual discernment.

Executive & reflective systems

  • Prefrontal cortex: planning, inhibition, attention, long-term goals.
  • Orbitofrontal / ventromedial PFC: reward, risk and social value.
  • Anterior cingulate cortex: conflict monitoring, error detection and regulation.

Emotion, memory & body

  • Amygdala: threat and emotional salience.
  • Hippocampus: memory and context.
  • Insula: internal bodily states, disgust and craving.
  • Hypothalamus: hunger, thirst, sexual drive, hormones and autonomic regulation.

Reward & habits

  • Basal ganglia / striatum: action selection, repetition and habit learning.
  • Nucleus accumbens / ventral striatum: motivation and reward anticipation.
  • Dopamine: reward prediction, salience, motivation and learning — not simply a “pleasure chemical.”

Awareness & arousal

  • Thalamus: sensory relay and participation in awareness.
  • Brainstem: wakefulness, breathing and basic arousal.
  • Default mode network: self-referential and autobiographical thought.

From trigger to habit — and where choice can enter

1. TriggerSituation, cue, memory, image or stressor.
2. Automatic responseBodily arousal, salience, learned association.
3. Thought / desireFear, anger, craving, attraction or interpretation.
4. Awareness“I notice what is happening.”
5. ConsiderationReasoning, inhibition, reappraisal, values.
6. Choice / actionConsent, refusal, delay or another response.
7. RepetitionRepeated actions can strengthen habits and dispositions.
Psychology and Catholic moral theology use related but different language. Emotions, temptations and involuntary reactions can arise before deliberate choice. Catholic moral reflection considers knowledge, freedom, consent, circumstances and the chosen act. Repetition can contribute to habits, virtues or vices, but pride, greed, lust, envy, gluttony, anger and sloth should never be reduced to one neural structure.

Psychology Foundations

No prior psychology study is required. Begin with research methods, then explore brain and body, attention, habits, memory, emotions, development and help-seeking. All exercises can be completed with fictional examples.

  1. Psychology as a scienceDistinguish evidence, interpretation and personal opinion.
  2. Brain, body and behaviourMap major systems for planning, memory, reward, emotion and bodily drives without assigning a vice to one brain region.
  3. Attention, consciousness and sleepDifferentiate conscious awareness, preconscious retrieval and automatic or unconscious processing.
  4. Learning and habitsExplain cue → behaviour → consequence loops, reward learning and gradual automaticity.
  5. Memory and effective studyDistinguish encoding, storage and retrieval.
  6. Emotion, stress and regulationUse simple coping skills without suppressing or moralising feelings.
  7. Development across the lifespanExplain variation without using a stage model as a diagnosis.
  8. Personality, social influence and cultureNotice situational explanations and avoid stereotyping.
  9. Mental health literacy and help-seekingRecognise when education should lead to qualified assessment.
  10. Integrating psychology, ethics and faithIntegrate psychology with freedom, consent, virtue and vice without reducing morality to brain chemistry.
